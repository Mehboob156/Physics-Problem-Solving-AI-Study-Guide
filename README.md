# Physics Problem Solving & AI Study Guide

Physics problems are often difficult for reasons that go beyond the final calculation. A student may understand a concept but struggle to translate a word problem into known quantities, identify the unknown, choose the correct physical principle, establish assumptions, or determine whether the final result makes sense.

This guide presents a structured approach to solving physics problems with AI-assisted study tools while keeping the reasoning visible and the calculations checkable.

## 1. Start by Interpreting the Problem

Before writing an equation, determine exactly what the question is asking.

A useful first step is to identify:

* What physical situation is being described?
* What quantities are given?
* What quantity must be found?
* What units are being used?
* Is there a diagram or direction that matters?
* Are any assumptions required?

For example, a motion problem might provide an initial velocity, acceleration, and time while asking for final velocity. Writing these quantities down first makes the mathematical structure of the problem much clearer.

The goal is to translate the words into a physical model before beginning the algebra.

## 2. Separate Known and Unknown Quantities

Create a simple list of the known quantities and the quantity you need to calculate.

For example:

**Known**

u = 5 m/s
a = 2 m/s²
t = 4 s

**Unknown**

v = ?

This simple step prevents a common mistake: selecting an equation before understanding what information the problem actually provides.

It is especially useful for longer word problems containing several numerical values, because not every number in a question is necessarily required for the final calculation.

## 3. State the Assumptions

Physics solutions often depend on assumptions about the physical situation.

Examples include:

* Air resistance is ignored.
* The surface is frictionless.
* Acceleration is constant.
* Gravitational acceleration is taken as 9.8 m/s².
* The object is treated as a point mass.
* The reference direction is chosen as positive.

Making these assumptions visible allows you to check whether the selected model actually represents the situation.

A hidden assumption can turn an apparently correct calculation into a physically incorrect answer.

## 4. Choose the Physical Principle

Once the known and unknown quantities are clear, identify the principle that connects them.

Depending on the problem, this might involve:

* Kinematic equations
* Newton's laws
* Conservation of energy
* Conservation of momentum
* Work-energy relationships
* Circular motion
* Electric fields
* Electric circuits
* Magnetic forces
* Wave relationships
* Simple harmonic motion
* Optics
* Fluid pressure and volume relationships

The important question is not simply “Which formula do I remember?” but “Which physical principle describes this situation?”

## 5. Write the Equation Before Substituting Numbers

Keep the symbolic equation separate from the numerical substitution.

For constant acceleration, for example:

**v = u + at**

Only after establishing the relationship should the values be substituted:

**v = 5 + (2)(4)**

Then:

**v = 13 m/s**

This structure makes the reasoning easier to inspect and makes algebraic mistakes easier to find.

It also makes the solution reusable when the values change.

## 6. Track Units Throughout the Calculation

Units are part of the physics, not decoration added after the calculation.

Check that the units are compatible before substituting values. After solving, confirm that the final unit corresponds to the quantity being calculated.

For example, velocity should have units of distance divided by time, while acceleration should have units of distance divided by time squared.

Dimensional analysis can catch many calculation errors, although dimensional consistency alone does not prove that a physical model is correct.

## 7. Treat Vectors as Vectors

Some physics quantities require direction as well as magnitude.

Velocity, acceleration, force, momentum, electric fields, and magnetic forces can all require vector treatment.

For example, a force of 10 N to the right is not equivalent to a force of 10 N to the left. Similarly, resolving a vector into components can make a two-dimensional problem much easier to solve.

When direction matters, define the coordinate system and sign convention before beginning the calculation.

## 8. Use Diagrams When They Clarify the Physics

A diagram can turn a complicated description into a much simpler physical model.

Useful visual representations include:

* Free-body diagrams
* Projectile-motion diagrams
* Vector-component diagrams
* Circuit diagrams
* Ray diagrams
* Wave diagrams
* Field diagrams
* Pressure-volume diagrams

A free-body diagram, for example, can help identify which forces act on an object before Newton's laws are applied.

The purpose of a diagram is not decoration. It should make the relationships between the physical quantities easier to understand.

## 9. Check the Result Independently

A numerical answer should not automatically be considered correct simply because the algebra looks reasonable.

Useful checks include:

* Recalculating the arithmetic
* Checking dimensions
* Comparing the result with the expected scale
* Substituting the answer back into the original relationship
* Checking the sign and direction
* Considering limiting cases
* Comparing with an alternative solution method when appropriate

Independent arithmetic checking is particularly useful when AI is involved. A language model can produce plausible-looking mathematical work while still making an arithmetic or modelling error.

## 10. What to Do When Information Is Missing

Do not automatically invent a missing value.

If a problem does not provide enough information, identify the missing quantity and determine whether it can legitimately be inferred from the wording or from an established physical condition.

For example, if a calculation requires a mass but the problem provides no mass and no relationship from which mass can be obtained, the correct response is to identify the missing information rather than silently choosing a value.

This is one of the most important habits when using AI for physics.

## 11. Physics Word Problems

Word problems require translation from natural language into physics.

A practical workflow is:

**Read → Identify the physical system → List knowns → Identify unknown → Draw a diagram → State assumptions → Select principle → Write equations → Substitute → Calculate → Check → Interpret**

This process separates physical reasoning from arithmetic.

The same approach works for problems involving motion, forces, energy, momentum, electricity, waves, and many other introductory physics topics.

## 12. Physics Equations

An equation can be used for more than calculating a final number.

You may need to:

* Rearrange an equation
* Solve for a particular variable
* Determine the meaning of each symbol
* Check dimensions
* Identify the conditions under which the equation applies
* Compare two related equations
* Determine how changing one variable affects another

For example, starting from:

**v = u + at**

you can rearrange it to solve for acceleration:

**a = (v - u) / t**

The algebra is only part of the task. Understanding when the equation applies is equally important.

## 13. Conceptual Physics Questions

Not every physics question requires a numerical answer.

Conceptual questions may ask why an object accelerates, why a force changes motion, how energy is transferred, or what happens when a particular variable changes.

For these questions, focus on the physical relationship rather than forcing the problem into a calculation.

A useful conceptual explanation should identify the principle, describe the relevant variables, and connect the principle to the physical situation.

## 14. Using AI as a Physics Tutor

AI can be useful for physics study when it exposes the reasoning instead of simply returning a final number.

A productive AI-assisted workflow can include four different approaches:

### Solve

Use a complete worked solution when you need to see the entire method, compare your result with a finished solution, or review a problem after attempting it yourself.

### Learn

Use a concept-focused explanation when you do not yet understand the underlying physics. A useful lesson can include a short concept summary, relevant quantities, equations, a real-world example, common mistakes, and a practice question.

### Tutor

Use hints and guiding questions when you are stuck but still want to complete the problem yourself. Instead of immediately revealing the final answer, the tutor can identify the next useful step.

### Check My Work

Use your own attempted solution as the starting point. The objective is to identify the earliest point where the reasoning breaks while preserving valid parts of your approach.

This can be more educational than replacing the entire solution with a different method.

## 15. Working with Photos, Diagrams and PDFs

Physics questions are often presented visually rather than as plain text.

A student may have:

* A photograph of a textbook problem
* A screenshot
* A handwritten calculation
* A free-body diagram
* A circuit
* A graph
* A multi-page PDF

When using an AI physics tool with visual input, check that the system correctly interprets the important numbers, symbols, labels, arrows, and directions.

If handwriting or an image is ambiguous, the safest approach is to confirm the interpretation before continuing.

## 16. Accuracy Has More Than One Meaning

Physics accuracy is not simply about whether the final number matches an answer key.

There are several possible failure points:

1. Misinterpreting the physical situation
2. Choosing the wrong physical model
3. Making an unreasonable assumption
4. Selecting an inappropriate equation
5. Making an algebraic mistake
6. Making an arithmetic error
7. Using inconsistent units
8. Reporting an incorrect sign or direction

A useful physics workflow should therefore make assumptions and intermediate reasoning visible instead of presenting only a final result.

## 17. Visualising Physics

Visuals can help connect mathematical equations to physical behaviour.

Useful visualisations include:

* Projectile motion
* Constant-acceleration motion
* Vector components
* Free-body diagrams
* Travelling and standing waves
* Simple harmonic motion
* Uniform circular motion
* Mechanical energy
* Electric fields
* Magnetic forces
* DC circuits
* Thin lenses
* Pressure-volume diagrams

An animation can be particularly useful when the important idea involves change over time.

For example, a projectile-motion visual can connect horizontal motion, vertical acceleration, velocity components, and the trajectory in a single representation.

## 18. Practice After Learning

Understanding a worked example does not necessarily mean that you can solve a new problem independently.

After studying a solution, attempt a related problem without looking at the answer.

A useful practice cycle is:

**Learn the concept → Study one example → Hide the solution → Attempt a new problem → Check your work → Identify the first mistake → Try again**

This turns passive solution reading into active problem solving.

## 19. Preparing for AP Physics

AP Physics preparation benefits from the same fundamentals used in general physics problem solving.

Focus on:

* Translating word problems into physical models
* Understanding equations rather than memorising them blindly
* Drawing appropriate diagrams
* Tracking units and directions
* Explaining physical reasoning
* Practising numerical and conceptual questions
* Reviewing mistakes systematically

## 20. A Practical Physics Problem-Solving Checklist

Before accepting a solution, ask:

* What is the problem actually asking?
* What quantities are known?
* What quantity is unknown?
* What assumptions am I making?
* Which physical principle applies?
* Did I choose an appropriate equation?
* Are my units consistent?
* Do directions and signs matter?
* Does the numerical result have a sensible magnitude?
* Can I independently check the calculation?
* Can I explain why the answer makes physical sense?

If you can answer these questions, you are doing more than calculating a number—you are solving the physics problem.

## Conclusion

Effective physics problem solving combines physical interpretation, mathematical reasoning, unit management, modelling, and verification.

AI can support that process, but the most useful approach is one that keeps the reasoning visible: identify the knowns and unknowns, state assumptions, select the physical principle, build the equations, calculate carefully, check the result, and understand what the answer means.
